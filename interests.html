Research interests and activities


My interests and research activities have been in two closely related fields:

  • computation of the electronic structure of matter (atoms, molecules and solids)


  and

  • applying this knowledge to interpreting results of various forms of ionization spectroscopies such as photoelectron, Auger electron, coincidence electron-electron and electron-photon spectroscopies.

Fundamentally, ionization spectroscopy is a natural way of looking at a many-electron system. This follows from taking an electron outside the system where its energy and momentum can be fully determined and then restoring its original state within the system by using universal conservation laws. In practice, however, this simple idea is hampered by collective behavior of the many-electron ensemble which tends to rearrange itself in response to removal of the particle. Thus a full description of the ionization process should include correlational effects in the system. Another complication is caused by various interactions of the ionized electron with the target and projectile particles. All this constitutes a very rich physical picture, the description of which is a real challenge for the theory. This description is also of great practical importance since various forms of ionization spectroscopy are widely used in numerous applications.

I started developing a theory of ionization in the simple limit of large energy transfer where the ionized electron leaves the system quickly and does not interact with it (Kheifets & Bray, 1998). In this limit the problem can be formulated in terms of the one-hole Green's function. I investigated analytical properties of the Green's function in the complex energy plane and found unique correspondence between its peculiarities and the positions and intensities of the main and satellite lines in the ionization spectrum. I formulated this correspondence in terms of universal spectroscopic sum rules which are equally valid for an arbitrary many-electron system. As an illustration, I performed numerical calculations for the ionization spectra of noble gas atoms, the results of which were in excellent agreement with experimental data.

The next development of the theory was the inclusion of ground state correlations in the system to be ionized. This development explained a profound difference that has existed between photoionization and electron impact ionization spectra from identical targets. The model clearly showed the experimental discrepancies were as a result of the different momentum transfer regimes of the experiments, which is equivalent to probing the target wave function at different areas of coordinate space. This resolved, in a simple and elegant way, a long standing controversy surrounding the observed spectral intensities, which involved many experimental and theoretical groups.

The subsequent development in the model meant going beyond the initial assumption of the high energy transfer, which is not realistic in many applications. The ionized electron, when it is slow, does interact with the system which results in changing its quantum state and also that of the system. This can be taken into account by applying the close-coupling computation scheme which allows for mixing various outgoing channels. This scheme was developed in momentum space and applied to atomic ionization with great success. In solids, the low energy outgoing electron can not be treated as a plane wave and more sophisticated low energy electron diffraction waves should be used instead. This type of calculation results in a complex pattern of electron impact ionization cross section of a solid, which is very sensitive to the bulk and surface crystalline structure.

During the past three years I have mostly concentrated on developing a unified computational approach and obtaining a wide range of consistent and very accurate numerical results for energy-resolved electron momentum density of various electron states in bulk crystalline and amorphous solids as well as solid surfaces, interfaces, man-made structures and defects. Materials of interest include various technologically important semiconductors, magnetic metals and alloys, and high-temperature superconductors. Until now the major theoretical efforts in electronic structure studies have concentrated on band energies calculations. However, it is the wave functions of the electrons that are generally much more sensitive to the crystal environment so that, provided they can be measured experimentally, they give a much greater insight into the origin and nature of the corresponding electron states.

In terms of a complete testing of the wave function, the energy-resolved electron momentum spectroscopy based on the (e,2e) reaction promises enormous potential. In this type of reaction a primary fast electron knocks out a bound electron from the solid and both secondary electrons are detected in time coincidence with fully determined kinematics. When the energy of the primary and two secondary electrons is large enough one can think of the (e,2e) reaction as a free electron-electron collision satisfying the energy and momentum conservation laws. In this way the spectral momentum density of the bound electrons can be determined. This provides a unique opportunity for direct mapping of the momentum space wave function in solids which has been demonstrated by the recent results of the Flinders group on various carbon allotropes, amorphous silicon, germanium, polycrystalline silicon carbide and corundum, monocrystalline silicon and natural graphite.

In my computations on the spectral momentum density in solids I employed the linear muffin-tin orbitals (LMTO) method with von Barth and Hedin parametrization for the exchange-correlation potential. Although the LMTO method is just one of many computational schemes derived within the general density functional philosophy, I found it advantageous in terms of accuracy and computational efficiency and capable of performing full-scale band structure calculations on solids with a large number of valence electrons per unit cell. In addition, the LMTO method employs a unified set of basis functions thus providing a conceptually consistent description for various physical systems.

At the moment the work on the wave function mapping, which has been successfully implemented for 3D bulk states, is extended to the surface states, interfaces, man-made structures and defects. This is a challenging and very important task since, with global miniaturization, such states play an increasingly vital role in the operation of solid state electronic devices. In my studies on the lower dimensional systems I employ the LMTO-based Green's function technique. In this approach the interaction in a chosen direction can be made extremely short ranged, and only a few atomic layers close to the object of interest (surface, interface, etc.) have to be treated self-consistently via a Dyson equation. This is in contrast to the traditional approach where standard three-dimensional methods are applied. Convergence of these methods requires enormous computational resources and precludes any meaningful practical applications.

The Green's function method has been tested so far on simple metal systems only. I intend to develop the method and make it applicable to a much wider range of materials. In these studies I collaborate with the group of Prof. Skriver of the Technical University of Denmark
